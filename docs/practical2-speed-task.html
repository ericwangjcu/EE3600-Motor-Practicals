Practical 2
DC Motor Speed Control with P and PI

Main learning task: explain why proportional control leaves a speed offset and how integral action removes it. Reuse the Practical 1 model, simulate P and PI, test a reviewed PI candidate, then verify three fresh repeats. Position is optional in simulation only.

1. Carry forward the Practical 1 model

Select your motor: 6 V / 280 RPM output / 21.3:1, or 6 V / 1360 RPM output / 4.4:1. Both use the same P2 UF2 and 44 decoded counts per motor revolution. The selector changes the gearbox conversion, not your measured model or controller gains.

Use your existing experimental gain K̄, time constant τ̄ and gearbox ratio N. Do not repeat identification, voltage/speed measurements, physical-parameter derivations or P1 model validation.

GP1(s) = K̄ / (τ̄s + 1)   [gearbox-output rad/s per volt]

The hardware page uses motor-shaft RPM and fractional duty u (0.40 = 40%). With the actual declared supply Vs and nominal approximation Vmotor ≈ Vsu:

Gn(s) = K / (τs + 1)
K = K̄ × N × 60/(2π) × Vs   [motor RPM / duty fraction]
τ = τ̄

The conversion changes the gain, not the time constant. K is the plant gain, not controller gain Kp. Keep your own motor/gearbox/load and record the supply and units. Driver losses and nonlinear behaviour are not separately represented by this voltage scaling.

2. Analyse and simulate P-only control

Use unity negative feedback, e = r − n, with C(s) = Kp. Derive the closed-loop response:

TP(s) = KKp / (τs + 1 + KKp)
τcl = τ / (1 + KKp)
n∞ = rKKp / (1 + KKp),   ess/r = 1 / (1 + KKp)
  1. Set Ki = Kd = 0. Simulate the same reference with at least three increasing P gains.
  2. Plot reference and speed; record final speed, offset, time constant and required duty.
  3. Explain why P needs a remaining error to supply the drive needed for steady rotation.

For this ideal first-order model, positive P gives a monotonic response with no overshoot. Increasing P speeds the response and reduces, but does not eliminate, the offset. Settling around the wrong final speed is not successful target tracking. A physical P-only trial is not required.

The control website's Compare P panel uses the ideal model. The separate legacy limited simulator includes a fixed actuator lag, so its P response need not have the same no-overshoot property.

3. Add I and tune PI in simulation

CPI(s) = Kp + Ki/s
TPI(s) = K(Kps + Ki) / [τs² + (1 + KKp)s + KKi]

I accumulates persistent error. At zero error, the accumulated integral contribution can remain nonzero and supply the required steady drive. Zero ideal step error assumes a stable loop and an achievable target.

  1. Choose a reasonable P gain. Hold P fixed initially, keep D = 0 and increase I from a small positive value.
  2. Compare at least three I settings. Inspect speed, duty, overshoot, rise, settling and final error.
  3. Refine P and I one at a time if necessary. Record the reason for each change and save the initial and selected designs.
  4. Check the complete response, including the PI zero at −Ki/Kp. Denominator-only overshoot formulas do not determine this complete response.
  5. Before hardware, check duty limits, the 10 ms controller interval and declared speed filtering. Do not add measurement lag twice if it is already included in the P1 fit.

PI is sufficient when it meets the requirements. D was not needed in the reported successful speed tests. No extra time-constant helper or pole-choice exercise is required.

4. Speed requirements and measurement conventions

Use one agreed achievable positive step from rest, with the same target, supply, load and limits throughout. The reported reference kit used 1000 motor RPM at 6 V; this is not a universal target for every kit.

QuantityRequirement
Overshoot≤5% of the commanded step, beyond the final target.
10–90% rise≤0.50 s. Also report command-to-10% time to expose startup delay.
Settling≤1.00 s from the command event; all later assessment samples in band, with ≥2 s final in-band dwell.
Final errorLast-2-second mean absolute error ≤ max(2% of target, 2q). Report signed bias too.
q = 60 / (0.050Cm) motor RPM
Settling band = ±max(0.02|rf − r0|, 2q), centred on rf

For Cm = 44 decoded counts per motor revolution, 2q = 54.545 motor RPM. At 1000 RPM this is ±5.45%, not strict ±2%. State which term governs the band. The ideal simulation comparison reports strict 2% separately. Faults, incomplete recordings or insufficient dwell cannot pass.

5. Website and Pico workflow

The website sections 1–6 follow this sheet. Only one section is shown at a time; use Next/Back or the numbered section buttons. The motor selector is in section 1.

  1. Section 1 — P1 model. Select your motor and actual supply, enter your own K̄ and τ, and choose the agreed target in motor RPM. Select Use my P1 model. The model is converted once for the following simulation sections; no gains are applied.
  2. Section 2 — P only. Enter a base P gain and compare 0.5×, 1× and 2× P. Save the simulated CSV and record the offset and speed of response.
  3. Section 3 — Add I. Keep the selected base P fixed; compare three I settings. Refine and justify your candidate. Use MATLAB/Simulink to check the declared limits and sampled behaviour before tutor review.
  4. Section 4 — Targets. Review the same response targets and encoder-aware band stated above.
  5. Section 5 — Pico test. Use desktop Chrome/Edge and the existing P2 UF2. Select Connect Pico, then Apply configuration while disarmed. The chosen profile sets N and motor CPR automatically; check the displayed readback and tutor-approved duty limit. Enter your reviewed Kp and Ki. D remains zero and the normal trial is 10 s.
  6. Arm & run speed trial. Inspect speed and duty. STOP removes drive; allow coast-down. A fault requires tutor diagnosis, not an automatic retry.
  7. Section 6 — Results. Select Analyse response → Save CSV. Save before another trial. Stop before changing a gain; document each refinement.
  8. Freeze and repeat three times. Retain every attempt, reload saved P2 CSVs and check reproducible metrics. Do not change gains between final repeats.

Kp is duty fraction/RPM and Ki is duty fraction/(RPM·s). The new comparison panel uses these same units. Only the separate legacy simulator uses percent duty/output RPM: divide those gains by 100 × N before hardware entry. Unit conversion alone does not match filtering or sampling.

Do not apply a 67% minimum-duty clamp or hidden starting boost. The report only establishes that 67% produced movement in that short check, not an exact threshold. A 100% ceiling was approved for the reported 6 V bench, not every kit. On a fault, stop and obtain tutor diagnosis before restarting. Physical position controls are not part of this practical.
